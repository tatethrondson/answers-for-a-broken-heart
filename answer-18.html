Answer 18 · What Do I Do With This Anger?

Am I allowed to be furious with God?

Your anger may not mean faith is gone. It may mean you are still talking to the One you believe should have done something different.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Honest
Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart
Explore this topic: Anger & Unanswered Prayer →

The short answer

Anger at God is not the opposite of faith.

Scripture contains prayers, laments, accusations, and cries from people who were deeply angry with God. Honest anger brought to Him is not the same as rejecting Him. In many cases, the very fact that you are still arguing with God means you still believe He is there and that what happened matters to Him.

If you only have 60 seconds

One truth · One Scripture · One prayer · One step
One truth

You can be angry with God and still be talking to God.

One Scripture · Psalm 62:8

“Pour out your heart before him: God is a refuge for us.”

Pray this

“God, I am angry. I do not want anger to drive me away from You, so I am bringing it to You.”

One next step

Pray the sentence you have been censoring. God already knows it; honesty keeps the conversation open.

If your pain has become dangerous or you are afraid you may hurt yourself, do not carry that moment alone. Use the immediate safety pathway →

There is a version of Christianity that seems comfortable with sadness but nervous around anger.

Tears are acceptable. Fury feels dangerous.

So people swallow it. They change the word. They say they are “confused” when they are actually angry, or “struggling” when what they really mean is, I am furious with God for letting this happen.

But God already knows what is in your heart. Hiding it from Him does not make you more spiritual. It only makes your prayers less honest.

Naomi did not clean up her anger.

When Naomi returned to Bethlehem after losing her husband and sons, the women who knew her called her by name.

She told them not to.

Ruth 1:20-21 · KJV
“Call me not Naomi, call me Mara: for the Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and the LORD hath brought me home again empty…”

Naomi did not blame fate. She spoke directly about what she believed God had allowed in her life.

Her words are raw.

And they are in the Bible.

Scripture does not protect God from the anger of wounded people.

The Bible gives angry faith a vocabulary.

Habakkuk asks how long he will cry and God will not hear. Jeremiah reaches moments of despair so deep that he curses the day he was born. Job demands an audience with God.

And then there is Psalm 88—a psalm that never really turns the corner.

Psalm 88:18 · KJV
“Lover and friend hast thou put far from me, and mine acquaintance into darkness.”

Many psalms move from lament to praise. Psalm 88 ends in the dark.

God let it remain that way.

That should matter to anyone who feels pressure to make every prayer end with a smile.

Sometimes faith sounds like praise. Sometimes faith sounds like refusing to stop talking in the dark.

Anger is not the same thing as unbelief.

There is a difference between bringing anger to God and deciding God is unworthy of trust because He will not submit to your explanation.

God does correct His people. Job is corrected. So are others throughout Scripture.

But correction is not rejection.

In fact, one of the striking things in Job is that when God finally addresses the situation, He has sharp words for Job’s friends—the men who spent chapter after chapter explaining suffering neatly.

Job 42:7 · KJV
“Ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath.”

Job had said plenty that needed correction. But he had said it to God.

He stayed in the conversation.

Your anger may reveal something about your faith.

Think about it.

You do not yell at an empty room expecting an answer. You do not demand justice from a universe you believe is indifferent. You get angry at God because, somewhere beneath the anger, you believe He is there, He is powerful, and this world should be different than it is.

That does not make every angry thought correct.

It does mean the anger itself may be less spiritually dangerous than the silence that comes when you stop talking to God altogether.

The anger does not worry me nearly as much as the day you stop bringing anything to Him at all.

If you feel ashamed of your anger

Do not add shame to pain. Tell God the truth. You are not protecting Him by pretending. He is not fragile. He can correct you without abandoning you, listen without being threatened, and hold emotions you do not know what to do with yet.

Anger still needs boundaries.

Saying anger at God can be honest faith is not the same thing as saying anger should control you.

Anger can expose what hurts. It can also wound other people if it is allowed to govern your words and choices.

Ephesians 4:26 · KJV
“Be ye angry, and sin not…”

The verse does not pretend anger never happens. It warns us not to hand anger the steering wheel.

Bring the fire somewhere safe—first to God, then when needed to a trusted pastor, friend, counselor, or professional who can help you carry it without letting it burn everything nearby.

What do I do with this tonight?

Say the sentence you have been afraid to say.

God, I am angry with You.

Then tell Him why.

Do not try to resolve it all in the same prayer. You are reopening a conversation, not writing the conclusion.

Read Ruth 1. Read Psalm 88. Read Job. Let Scripture prove that the Bible has room for people whose prayers do not sound polished.

So here is Answer #18, stated plainly: being furious with God does not automatically mean you have lost your faith. Honest anger brought to Him can be evidence that the relationship is still alive. Keep talking. Let Him meet you in the anger, correct what needs correcting, and hold what you cannot yet resolve.

Questions that usually come next

Is anger at God a sin?

Feeling anger is not the same thing as deciding God is evil, refusing correction, or using pain as permission to sin. Scripture records deeply emotional prayers from Job, David, Habakkuk, Jeremiah, and others. They questioned. They protested. They lamented. The Bible does not edit those prayers out.

But biblical honesty is not the same as making anger our final authority. Faith brings anger into God’s presence and lets truth speak back. You can tell God, “I hate what happened. I do not understand why You allowed it. I am angry,” while still remaining open to His character, His Word, and His correction.

“How long, O LORD? wilt thou forget me for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?”Psalm 13:1 · KJV

Will God reject me because I am angry with Him?

The Psalms suggest the opposite response: bring the real heart to God rather than disappearing from Him. God already knows what you feel. The danger is not that He will discover your anger; the danger is allowing anger to become distance, bitterness, or a settled refusal to trust anything He says.

Wounded faith can still be faith. Sometimes the most faithful thing you can do is keep talking to God while you are hurt.

What do I pray when I am too angry to pray?

Start with one honest sentence. “God, I am angry because I wanted You to stop this.” Then tell Him what you lost, what you expected, what feels unfair, and what you are afraid your pain means about Him. You do not need to manufacture calm before you pray.

If you need words for that moment, What do I even say to God right now? is written specifically for the prayer you cannot polish.

What if the anger does not go away quickly?

Do not confuse an emotion that returns with a moral decision to stay bitter. Grief and anger often come in waves. Keep bringing the same wound into the light. Talk with wise people. Pay attention to whether anger is moving toward honesty, lament, wisdom, and surrender—or whether it is becoming isolation, contempt, revenge, and hardness.

A helpful distinction: anger can be a signal that something precious was lost or something wrong occurred. Let it tell you where the wound is, but do not let it become the only voice that tells you who God is.
