Answer 15 · Learning to Grieve

How long am I allowed to still be sad about this?

Grief is not a spiritual failure, and Scripture never puts a stopwatch on sorrow.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Gentle
Written by Tate Throndson · Senior pastor of Castleview Baptist Church in Castle Rock, Colorado · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart
Explore this topic: Grief & Loss →

The short answer

You’re allowed to grieve as long as it takes.

The Bible does not command you to hurry through grief. It gives grieving people language, room, tears, lament, community, and hope. Healing can be slow, uneven, and deeply personal. Still hurting does not mean you are failing God.

If you only have 60 seconds

One truth · One Scripture · One prayer · One step
One truth

There is no stopwatch on grief.

One Scripture · Psalm 56:8

“Thou tellest my wanderings: put thou my tears into thy bottle...”

Pray this

“God, receive the grief I am still carrying. Keep me from shaming myself for missing someone I loved.”

One next step

Give yourself permission to be sad today without measuring whether you should be over it by now.

If your pain has become dangerous or you are afraid you may hurt yourself, do not carry that moment alone. Use the immediate safety pathway →

One of the strangest things about grief is that the person grieving often feels pressure to make everyone else comfortable. At first people ask how you are doing because they know you are hurting. Later, sometimes without meaning to, they begin asking as though the answer ought to have changed by now.

Are you doing better?

Maybe you are. Maybe you are not. Maybe yesterday was easier and today feels like the first week all over again.

That does not mean you are going backward. It means grief does not move in a straight line.

Healing is not a staircase. Sometimes it circles back through rooms you thought you had already left.

The Bible does not put grief on a timer.

The Psalms are filled with people who are still hurting. Not people writing years later after everything resolved. People inside the pain, talking to God from there.

Psalm 13:1-2 · KJV
“How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily?”

David repeats the question because the pain repeats itself. Scripture does not edit out that repetition. God preserved it.

Psalm 42 says, “My tears have been my meat day and night.” That is not a passing bad afternoon. That is sorrow that has become part of the rhythm of daily life.

And God calls these words Scripture.

“God won’t give you more than you can handle” is not what the Bible says.

This sentence is usually offered kindly. But the verse people have in mind, 1 Corinthians 10:13, is about temptation—not about the maximum amount of sorrow a human being can feel.

1 Corinthians 10:13 · KJV
“There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful… but will with the temptation also make a way to escape.”

That passage promises faithfulness in temptation. It does not promise that grief will always feel manageable.

There are moments in Scripture when faithful people are overwhelmed. Paul says elsewhere that he was “pressed out of measure, above strength.” The point was not that he had enough strength in himself. The point was that he learned to depend on the God who raises the dead.

Sometimes life gives you more than you can handle so that you discover you were never meant to handle it alone.

Jesus did not rush sorrow.

At Lazarus’s tomb, Jesus already knew what He was about to do. Resurrection was minutes away.

And He still wept.

He did not tell Mary and Martha that their tears were unnecessary because the ending was about to change. He let grief be grief.

John 11:35 · KJV
“Jesus wept.”

If the Son of God did not skip sorrow even when He knew resurrection was coming, you do not have to apologize for feeling sorrow while you wait for yours.

Mourning is not a lack of faith.

Matthew 5:4 · KJV
“Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.”

Jesus does not say, “Blessed are they who recover quickly.” He blesses those who mourn.

Comfort is promised. Speed is not.

Romans 12 tells the church to “weep with them that weep.” That command assumes grief sometimes lasts long enough that we need people willing to remain present instead of trying to hurry us toward closure.

If you are hurting right now

You are allowed to have a day that feels worse than yesterday. You are allowed to cry after you thought you were finished crying. You are allowed to miss someone years later. You are allowed to need help. None of those things automatically mean your faith is weak. Grief is what love feels like when someone or something precious is gone.

Grief needs room—but it also needs movement.

Permission to grieve is not the same thing as permission to disappear forever inside the grief. You do not need a deadline, but you do need somewhere for the sorrow to go.

That may mean prayer. It may mean talking with a trusted friend. It may mean a pastor, counselor, physician, or support group. It may mean sleeping, eating, walking outside, returning to church, or simply making it through one more day.

The goal is not “getting over it.”

The goal is learning how to carry what happened without letting what happened become the only thing you can carry.

Grief does not need to be hurried. It does need to keep moving.

What do I do with this tonight?

Stop grading your grief.

You do not need to compare your timeline to someone else’s. You do not need to decide whether you “should” be doing better. Instead, ask one smaller question: What would faithfulness look like for me tonight?

Maybe it is opening Psalm 13 and letting David give you words. Maybe it is texting one safe person and admitting this is still hard. Maybe it is letting yourself cry instead of apologizing for the tears.

And if your grief has become so heavy that you cannot function, sleep, eat, or stay safe, getting professional help is not a failure of faith. It can be one of the wisest ways of refusing to carry alone what was never meant to be carried alone.

So here is Answer #15, stated plainly: Scripture gives grief room to last. You are not failing God because you still hurt. Let grief have the time it needs—and keep carrying it toward God, toward safe people, and toward the next faithful step.

Questions that usually come next

Does the Bible give grief a timetable?

No single timetable is given for every loss. Scripture includes seasons of mourning, tears that return, anniversaries of sorrow, and people whose lives were permanently changed by what happened. Ecclesiastes simply says there is “a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance.”

“A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance.”Ecclesiastes 3:4 · KJV

Healthy grief moves, but movement is not the same as speed. The goal is not to prove you are over it. The goal is to keep walking with God and people while learning how to carry a loss that may always matter.

Does a hard day mean I am going backward?

Not necessarily. Grief can be stirred by a date, song, place, smell, holiday, photograph, or ordinary moment you did not see coming. A wave of sadness after several better weeks does not automatically erase the progress that came before it.

Grief often moves more like a winding road than a straight line.

What if people think I should be over it by now?

Other people often become uncomfortable with grief before the grieving person is finished carrying it. Their discomfort does not establish a biblical deadline for your sorrow. You can be gracious with people who do not know what to say without accepting pressure to perform recovery for them.

At the same time, grief should not become a reason to isolate indefinitely from every relationship, responsibility, or source of help. You are allowed to move slowly, but you were not meant to carry grief alone.

How do I know whether grief is becoming stuck?

There is no simple test, but pay attention to direction. Are you still able, over time, to receive love, talk honestly, remember the person or loss with more than one emotion, pray, and remain connected to life? Or is grief increasingly hardening into isolation, bitterness, revenge, hopelessness, or complete withdrawal?

If you are worried that the pain is becoming heavier instead of simply different, Why does grief feel worse over time? goes directly into that question.

You are not on a stopwatch. Healing is not measured by how quickly you stop missing someone. It is seen in whether sorrow is slowly learning to coexist with love, hope, truth, and life.
