Faith and mental health

Can Christians go to counseling?

Yes. Seeking wise help is not automatically a lack of faith. Prayer, Scripture, the church, wise pastoral care, skilled counseling, and appropriate medical care do not have to be competitors.

Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart

The short answer

A Christian should never surrender biblical truth to a counselor, but neither should a Christian assume that asking for skilled help means he has stopped trusting God. God often cares for us through people He has equipped to help us see, understand, and address what we cannot untangle alone.

I have met Christians who were willing to see a cardiologist for their heart, a surgeon for their knee, and a dentist for a tooth—but felt guilty about seeing a counselor when their thoughts, emotions, marriage, trauma, or depression became more than they knew how to carry. Somewhere along the way, they had absorbed the idea that a truly spiritual person should be able to pray harder, read more Scripture, and get through it alone.

I do not believe the Bible teaches that.

“Where no counsel is, the people fall: but in the multitude of counsellors there is safety.”Proverbs 11:14 · KJV

That verse is not a technical endorsement of modern psychotherapy. It is something broader and simpler: God does not present isolation as wisdom. Throughout Scripture, wise people receive counsel, correction, care, instruction, and help from others.

Needing help is not the opposite of trusting God. Sometimes asking for help is one of the ways we trust Him.

Prayer and counseling do not have to compete.

If someone told you, “Do not pray; just go to counseling,” I would disagree. But I would also disagree with, “Do not get counseling; just pray.” Those choices create a conflict the Bible does not require us to create.

We pray because God is God. We search Scripture because His Word is true. We stay connected to the church because believers were never designed to suffer alone. And there are times when we also receive help from someone trained to recognize patterns of trauma, anxiety, depression, grief, addiction, relational dysfunction, or other mental-health concerns.

A counselor cannot do what only Christ can do. A pastor cannot do everything a licensed clinician is trained to do. A physician is not a replacement for the local church. The healthiest care often begins when we stop demanding that one person or one form of help do every job.

What kind of help do I need?

Not every struggle requires the same kind of care. Sometimes you primarily need a mature Christian friend who will listen and tell you the truth. Sometimes you need your pastor to help you think biblically through guilt, suffering, marriage, forgiveness, doubt, or a spiritual decision. Sometimes a skilled biblical counselor may be a good fit. Sometimes what you are carrying calls for a licensed mental-health professional, medical evaluation, or both.

Pastoral care

Especially helpful when the primary questions are spiritual, theological, relational, or connected to discipleship, grief, guilt, prayer, forgiveness, and biblical decision-making.

Professional counseling or therapy

Can be especially helpful when symptoms are persistent, daily functioning is being affected, trauma is involved, relationships are repeatedly breaking down, or you need structured clinical tools and assessment.

Medical care

May be important when physical conditions, medication questions, severe symptoms, sleep changes, appetite changes, or other medical factors may be contributing. A qualified medical professional can evaluate those issues.

These categories can overlap. You do not have to choose between having a pastor and having a therapist. In a healthy situation, spiritual care and professional care can communicate respectfully without pretending to be the same thing.

Does counseling mean Scripture is not enough?

This question usually comes from a sincere desire to honor the Bible, and that matters. Scripture is sufficient for what God intends Scripture to do. It reveals God, tells us the truth about sin and salvation, renews our minds, teaches us how to live, and points us to Christ. But biblical sufficiency does not mean the Bible is the only source of information Christians may ever use.

The Bible does not teach you how to repair a transmission, diagnose a thyroid disorder, or set a broken bone. Using trained help in those areas does not insult Scripture. In the same way, learning clinically informed tools for trauma, panic, addiction, communication, or emotional regulation does not automatically mean you have replaced the Bible. The question is whether the help you receive is being treated as a substitute for God or as a tool under His truth.

What should I look for in a counselor?

Do not simply ask, “Is this person a Christian?” Ask whether the person is competent for the problem you are bringing, whether you understand their approach, whether they respect your convictions, and whether you can speak openly about faith without being mocked or pressured to abandon biblical beliefs.

If you specifically want Christian counseling, ask direct questions before you begin. How does your faith inform your work? How do you approach Scripture? What are your credentials? What experience do you have with the issue I am facing? What does confidentiality cover, and what are its legal limits? A trustworthy professional should be able to explain the scope of their work clearly.

What if I have been told counseling is a sign of weak faith?

Then I would ask you to look at what Scripture actually celebrates. Moses received practical counsel from Jethro when he was overwhelmed. Proverbs repeatedly commends wise counsel. Paul traveled and ministered in community. The church is pictured as a body whose members need one another. The biblical picture of maturity is not radical self-sufficiency.

“Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.”Galatians 6:2 · KJV

There is a kind of pride that refuses help because we are afraid of what needing help says about us. Humility can sound like, “I do not know how to carry this by myself anymore.”

What about medication?

Medication is a medical question, and it deserves medical guidance rather than shame or sweeping spiritual statements. Some Christians have strong opinions in every direction. A pastor can help you think spiritually and ethically, but decisions about starting, changing, or stopping psychiatric medication should be discussed with an appropriately qualified medical professional who knows your history and symptoms.

Taking medication does not prove your faith is weak, and taking medication does not solve every spiritual or emotional issue. It is one possible part of care for some people. Do not make a major medication change based only on an internet article, a friend’s experience, or a sermon.

So where should I begin?

Begin with honesty. What is happening? How long has it been happening? Is it interfering with work, sleep, relationships, worship, school, eating, concentration, or your ability to function? Have you become increasingly isolated? Are you using substances or other behaviors to numb what you feel? Are you afraid you may hurt yourself or someone else?

Then tell somebody trustworthy. Your first conversation might be with a pastor, doctor, counselor, spouse, parent, or mature Christian friend. You do not have to know the perfect category before you ask for help.

Faith does not require you to pretend you are fine. Faith brings the truth into the light and asks God for wisdom about the next right step.
If you feel unsafe, believe you may harm yourself or someone else, or are facing an immediate mental-health emergency, do not rely on an article or wait for a routine appointment. Use the site’s immediate safety pathway and seek urgent local help.
