Answer 16 · Learning to Grieve

Why did this happen to me?

Sometimes “why?” is the only honest question you have. But there may come a day when a different question helps you move again.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Forward-looking
Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart
Explore this topic: Grief & Loss →

The short answer

When you’re ready, ask a different question.

You may never receive a complete explanation for why this happened. But when the time is right, shifting from “Why did this happen?” to “What can God do with this?” can turn your face from an unchangeable past toward a future God can still redeem.

If you only have 60 seconds

“Why did this happen to me?” is not a foolish question. It is often the first question grief asks.

Why this diagnosis? Why this accident? Why this betrayal? Why my child? Why now?

There is a season when you should be allowed to ask it without anyone trying to hurry you out of it. We have already seen that Scripture makes room for lament, confusion, and sorrow.

But there is something else I have learned: why can become a room with no door if it becomes the only question you are willing to ask.

Why can look backward forever.

The problem is not that “why?” is sinful. The problem is that some questions can only turn over the same ground.

You can replay the conversation. Reconstruct the accident. Reread the medical report. Imagine a different decision. Ask what you missed and what might have changed if one detail had gone another way.

Sometimes that work is useful. Sometimes it helps you understand. But there are losses where no amount of replay can produce the answer your heart wants.

A question can be honest and still become a prison if it never lets you face forward.

The question that changed the direction.

Years ago, in one of the hardest seasons of our family’s life, a woman named Ann Gibbs gave my wife and me counsel that stayed with me.

Ann knew long-term physical pain herself. Her words did not come from a plaque on a wall. They came from a life that had wrestled with suffering, anger, and faith.

She told us, in essence: stop asking only why this happened. Start asking what God is going to do with it.

I want to protect that sentence from becoming another cliché.

It is not something to throw at a grieving person three days after the funeral.

It is not a demand that you find a silver lining.

It is not permission for anyone else to decide when you should be ready.

But when you are ready, it can open a door.

“Why?” asks for an explanation. “What now?” asks God for a future.

Scripture repeatedly turns suffering toward what God can still do.

Joseph looked back on betrayal, slavery, false accusation, and years of loss and said something astonishing to the brothers who had started so much of it.

Genesis 50:20 · KJV
“But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good…”

Joseph did not call their evil good. He named it evil.

But he also refused to believe their evil had the final word.

That is the distinction we need. Christianity never requires you to relabel the wound. It asks whether God may still be able to write beyond it.

Romans 8:28 · KJV
“And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.”

Paul does not say all things are good. He says God can work through all things toward good.

What might God do with this?

The answer will not be identical for everyone.

Maybe the wound will deepen your compassion. Maybe it will make you notice people you used to walk past. Maybe you will become the safe person you once needed. Maybe God will use what you learned to protect someone else. Maybe the way you endure will become a witness. Maybe there will be fruit you cannot see for years.

And maybe tonight you cannot see any of that yet.

That is okay.

The new question does not require an immediate answer. It simply turns the direction of your heart.

If you are not ready to ask “what” yet

Then do not force it. “I don’t know yet” is a legitimate place to be. Grief is not healed by pretending to be further down the road than you are. Let God meet you where you actually are. The point is not speed. The point is that someday, when you are ready, there is another question available.

Facing forward is not forgetting.

Moving forward does not mean leaving the person you lost behind. It does not mean excusing what someone did. It does not mean pretending the diagnosis never happened.

It means accepting that the past is fixed while the future is still open.

You cannot rewrite yesterday.

But God is still writing tomorrow.

The event may be finished. God’s work with the event may not be.

What do I do with this tonight?

Keep your “why” if you still need it. God can handle it.

But put another question beside it: Lord, I do not understand why this happened. What would You have me do with what remains?

Do not demand a five-year plan. Ask for the next faithful step.

Who needs your honesty? What relationship needs attention? What help do you need? What part of your heart have you been afraid to give God because giving it to Him would mean admitting the story is still moving?

So here is Answer #16, stated plainly: you may never receive a complete answer to “why did this happen to me?” But when you are ready, begin asking, “What can God do with this?” The first question stares at a closed door. The second remembers that God can still open one ahead of you.

Questions that usually come next

Why did this happen to me?

Sometimes there is a direct answer: a decision had consequences, another person acted wrongly, a body became sick, or an accident occurred. But even when we know the immediate cause, the deeper question remains: Why was this allowed to become part of my story?

Scripture does not give a personalized hidden reason for every painful event. That means we should be very cautious about inventing one. Not every tragedy is a secret punishment, not every loss is a divine lesson we can identify, and not every wound arrives with an explanation.

“And his disciples asked him, saying, Master, who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind? Jesus answered, Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents...”John 9:2–3 · KJV

Did this happen because I did something wrong?

Possibly some suffering is connected to our choices, but pain itself is not proof of personal guilt. Job’s friends assumed suffering meant Job had committed some hidden offense, and Scripture exposes how badly they misread him. Jesus rejected the disciples’ simplistic attempt to reduce one man’s suffering to a blame equation.

If there is something to confess, confess it. But do not manufacture guilt merely because you hurt.

Why me—and not someone else?

That comparison rarely produces peace because there is no distribution chart of suffering that we are able to inspect. Pain feels especially unjust when we watch others live the version of life we wanted. Scripture gives permission to grieve that disparity without promising that we will understand it.

The more useful question is not, “What did I do to deserve a life different from theirs?” but, “Given the life I actually have, where is God inviting me to live faithfully now?”

What is a better question than “Why me?”

Not because “why” is forbidden, but because it can become a room with no door. Try adding questions that create movement: “What do I need now?” “Who can walk with me?” “What is this revealing about my heart?” “What kind of person do I want to become?” “Where could God still bring redemption?”

You are allowed to keep the why-question. You simply do not have to let it be the only question in the room.
