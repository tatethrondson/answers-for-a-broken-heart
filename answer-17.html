Answer 17 · Grief & Loss

Why does it feel like I’m getting worse instead of better?

A hard day does not automatically mean you are losing ground. Grief often moves in waves, and slow healing is still healing.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Practical
Written by Tate Throndson · Senior pastor of Castleview Baptist Church in Castle Rock, Colorado · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart
Explore this topic: Grief & Loss →

The short answer

Healing is not a straight line.

A hard day does not automatically mean you are losing ground. Grief often moves in waves. The question is not whether you still hurt, but whether the hurt is slowly being brought into truth, grace, relationship, and wise care rather than hardening into isolation or bitterness.

If you only have 60 seconds

One truth · One Scripture · One prayer · One step
One truth

Healing is not always linear, but pain must be allowed to move.

One Scripture · Ecclesiastes 3:4

“A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance.”

Pray this

“God, help me grieve honestly without making grief my permanent home.”

One next step

Notice one small sign of movement: a conversation, a walk, a laugh, a prayer, a moment of rest. Do not dismiss it.

If your pain has become dangerous or you are afraid you may hurt yourself, do not carry that moment alone. Use the immediate safety pathway →

There is a difference between grief and bitterness, even though they can feel similar from the inside.

Grief hurts.

Bitterness hardens.

Grief can cry, question, rage, and still be moving. Bitterness takes that same pain and lets it settle so deeply that eventually everything starts tasting like it.

Grief is fire. Bitterness is ash.

The question is not whether you hurt. It is whether the hurt is still moving.

Psalm 13 is full of distress, but David is still talking to God. Job says things that make polite religious people uncomfortable, but he keeps addressing the God he is upset with. The Psalms of lament are proof that emotional intensity is not the same thing as spiritual collapse.

You can be furious and still be moving toward God.

You can be confused and still be moving toward God.

You can be heartbroken and still be moving toward God.

The motion matters.

Bitterness grows underground.

Hebrews 12:15 · KJV
“Looking diligently lest any man fail of the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled.”

Scripture calls bitterness a root. That is a powerful picture because roots do their work before anyone sees them.

A root grows quietly. It goes down before it comes up. And by the time bitterness finally appears in words, relationships, cynicism, anger, or withdrawal, it may have been feeding underground for a long time.

Notice also that Hebrews says “many” can be affected. Bitterness rarely stays contained.

Pain that has nowhere safe to go often finds another place to live.

How do I know whether grief is hardening?

There is no perfect diagnostic list, but a few questions can help.

Can you still talk honestly about the pain with God, or have you stopped talking to Him altogether? Can you receive comfort without resenting it? Can you imagine any future that is not defined by what happened? Are you isolating more and more? Has the wound become the lens through which you now interpret every person and every circumstance?

None of those questions are meant to shame you. They are meant to help you notice whether the fire is still moving or whether the ash is beginning to settle.

Staying in motion can look very ordinary.

It can look like praying an honest prayer instead of the polished one.

It can look like telling one trusted person what you are actually carrying.

It can look like meeting with a pastor, counselor, physician, or therapist when the weight is interfering with sleep, appetite, relationships, work, or your ability to function.

It can look like returning to Scripture even when you feel nothing.

It can look like getting out of bed, taking a walk, eating something, answering a text, showing up to church, or making one appointment you have been putting off.

Small motion is still motion.

If you feel ashamed that you are not “better” yet

Do not confuse slow healing with no healing. Grief loops. It stalls. It revisits old places. You may be further down the road than you realize simply because you are still willing to keep walking it honestly. The goal is not a pain-free life. The goal is a heart that remains open to God instead of hardening against Him.

Grace gives you somewhere to take the pain.

Bitterness often grows when pain feels trapped. The gospel gives pain a destination.

You can bring injustice to the God who judges rightly. You can bring loss to the Savior who wept. You can bring anger to the Father who already knows it is there. You can bring weakness to the One who says His grace is sufficient.

Psalm 62:8 · KJV
“Trust in him at all times; ye people, pour out your heart before him: God is a refuge for us.”

Pour it out. That is movement.

Not hide it. Not varnish it. Not pretend it is gone.

Pour it out.

What do I do with this tonight?

Ask yourself one question: Where has my grief stopped moving?

Maybe there is a conversation you need to have. Maybe there is help you need to ask for. Maybe you need to say the angry prayer you keep swallowing. Maybe you need to admit that the wound is touching more of your life than you wanted to acknowledge.

Then take one step.

Not ten. One.

So here is Answer #17, stated plainly: grief does not become unhealthy merely because it lasts. Healing often comes in waves. What matters is that you keep giving the pain somewhere honest and safe to go—toward God, truth, trustworthy people, and wise care—rather than letting isolation close in around you.

Questions that usually come next

Why can grief feel worse after the first few weeks?

Sometimes the earliest days are full of people, decisions, meals, phone calls, arrangements, and sheer survival. Later the house gets quieter. Other people return to normal routines. The reality of what has changed can become more visible when the activity around the loss begins to fade.

That does not automatically mean you are getting worse. Sometimes it means you are finally feeling what the first days did not give you room to feel.

Does a harder season mean I am grieving wrong?

No. Grief is not a straight line. Anniversaries, birthdays, holidays, places, smells, songs, photographs, and ordinary moments can reopen pain you thought had become more manageable. A difficult week after several better weeks does not erase the progress that came before it.

A wave returning does not mean the ocean has carried you back to the beginning.

Is deep grief the same thing as bitterness?

No. This distinction is important. Strong sorrow, recurring tears, anger, exhaustion, or missing someone intensely are not automatically bitterness. Bitterness is less about how much something hurts and more about what we begin doing with the hurt—whether it hardens into a settled posture of resentment, revenge, contempt, or refusal to let truth speak into the wound.

Do not shame yourself for grieving deeply. The goal is not to make the pain smaller on command. The goal is to keep the pain moving toward God, truthful community, wise help, and the next faithful step instead of letting it close every door around you.

What does healthy movement look like when I still hurt?

Movement can be very small. Getting out of bed. Eating something. Returning a phone call. Going to church. Taking a walk. Saying the person’s name. Talking honestly with a trusted friend. Making an appointment with a counselor or physician when you need more help. Progress is not measured only by whether you cried today.

You can still miss someone terribly and also begin participating in life again. Those realities are not betrayals of each other.

When should I stop trying to carry grief by myself?

You were never meant to carry it entirely by yourself. If grief is becoming increasingly isolating, making ordinary life feel impossible, or leaving you afraid you may not stay safe, involve other people now—a trusted friend, pastor, counselor, physician, or another appropriate professional. Asking for help is not admitting defeat. It is refusing isolation.

One important correction: “Grief that stops moving becomes bitterness” is a warning about what pain can become, not a diagnosis of everyone whose grief lasts a long time. Long grief is not automatically bitter grief.
