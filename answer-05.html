Answer 05 · Why Did This Happen?

Is it wrong that I keep asking God why?

God is not intimidated by the question you are afraid to ask. Scripture is filled with faithful people who brought Him their confusion instead of pretending they had none.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Honest

The short answer

Honest questions are not unbelief.

No. Asking God why does not automatically mean you have stopped trusting Him. Job asked. David asked. John the Baptist asked. God welcomes the honest cry of a hurting heart that still turns toward Him—even when the answer does not come quickly.

John asked too.

Some people are afraid that asking God why is itself a kind of spiritual failure. They imagine mature faith as a place where questions disappear and strong Christians simply accept whatever happens without wrestling. Scripture gives us a very different picture.

John the Baptist had seen more evidence than most of us will ever see. He baptized Jesus. He watched the Spirit descend. He had already announced Christ to Israel as the Lamb of God. And yet when Herod put him in prison and the days turned into weeks, the man who had pointed everyone else toward Jesus found himself needing reassurance.

Matthew 11:3 · KJV
“Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?”

That question came from a prison cell, not a philosophy classroom. John was not playing games with God. His circumstances had collided with what he thought the Messiah would do, and he needed to know whether he had misunderstood the whole thing.

Pain can make a truth you once preached feel like a question you now have to ask again.

Notice what Jesus did with the question.

Jesus did not shame John. He did not send back, “You should know better.” He pointed John toward what God was already doing—the blind seeing, the lame walking, the poor hearing the gospel—and then gave him a gentle invitation to keep trusting even when his personal story still hurt.

Matthew 11:6 · KJV
“And blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me.”

John’s prison door did not open. His story did not resolve the way he wanted. Yet immediately after John’s messengers left, Jesus spoke publicly about him with extraordinary honor. The unanswered question did not cancel the faithfulness of the man who asked it.

That matters if you are carrying a question you are embarrassed to say out loud. Jesus knows the difference between a cynical demand for proof and the cry of a wounded person trying to keep holding on.

Lament is not unbelief.

The Bible is full of people who brought God questions that would sound uncomfortable in a church hallway. David asked how long God would forget him. Job demanded an audience. Jeremiah poured out anguish. Habakkuk asked why God seemed to tolerate what was evil.

Psalm 13:1–2 · KJV
“How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily?”

Those words are in the Bible because God is not threatened by a prayer that tells the truth.

Lament is faith refusing to let go.

A person who has stopped believing God is there has no reason to keep talking to Him. Lament keeps the conversation going. It says, “I do not understand You right now. I am hurt. I am confused. But I am still bringing this to You.”

There is a difference between a question and an ultimatum.

Not every question is the same. One kind says, “God, unless You explain Yourself to my satisfaction, I refuse to trust You.” That puts us in the judge’s chair and God in the witness box. But another kind says, “Father, I do not understand what You are doing, and I need help trusting You here.”

The second question may sound messy. It may come with tears, frustration, even anger. But it is still relational. It is still turned toward God.

If you are afraid of your own questions

You do not have to sanitize them before you pray. Bring God the real question. He already knows it is there. Faith is not pretending you have no doubt; sometimes faith sounds like, “Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief.”

What do I do with this tonight?

Write the question you have been afraid to ask. Do not make it prettier. Pray it exactly as it is. Then read Psalm 13 and notice that David moves from anguish toward trust without pretending the anguish never existed.

You may not receive the explanation tonight. John did not. But you can give the question somewhere to live—in the presence of a God who already knows you are asking it.

So here is Answer #5, stated plainly: honest questions brought to God in faith are not unbelief. They are often the language of a wounded faith that is still reaching for Him.
