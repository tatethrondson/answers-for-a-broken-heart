Answer 09 · Does He Understand What This Feels Like?

Does God actually know what this feels like?

Your pain is specific. Your grief is personal. Christianity does not give you a God who watches it from a safe distance.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Honest

The short answer

He wept with you.

Jesus did not merely study suffering from heaven. He entered a human body, lived in a broken world, stood beside graves, felt betrayal and rejection, and wept with people He loved. He knows grief from the inside. You are not bringing your pain to a God who has never felt its weight.

If you only have 60 seconds

One truth · One Scripture · One prayer · One step
One truth

Jesus knows grief from the inside.

One Scripture · John 11:35

“Jesus wept.”

Pray this

“Jesus, thank You for entering human sorrow. Sit with me in mine.”

One next step

Picture Christ at Lazarus's grave. Let yourself grieve without apologizing for it.

One of the loneliest sentences a hurting person can say is, Nobody understands what this feels like. And in one sense, you may be right. No two people carry exactly the same loss. Nobody else had your relationship, your history, your hopes, your memories, or your particular version of the future that disappeared when this happened.

That is why comparisons rarely help. Someone can have walked through something similar and still not know your exact road. Suffering is lonely because suffering is specific.

So the question matters: when Christians say God understands, do we merely mean He knows all the facts? Or does He actually know what grief feels like?

Grief was not a rumor to Jesus.

Isaiah 53:3 · KJV
“He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief.”

Acquainted. Not briefed on. Not informed about. Acquainted—the way you become acquainted with a road you have walked enough times to know every turn. Scripture does not describe Jesus as a detached observer of human sorrow. It calls Him a man of sorrows.

He knew what it was to be misunderstood by His own family. He knew betrayal by a friend. He knew exhaustion, hunger, rejection, injustice, and loneliness. He knew what it felt like to pray while His closest friends fell asleep. He knew what it felt like to be abandoned by people who had promised they never would.

Grief was not something Jesus explained. It was something He carried.

Then He stood at a grave.

John 11 may be the clearest window in all of Scripture into the heart of God toward a hurting person. Lazarus was dead. Mary and Martha were grieving. And Jesus knew something nobody else standing there knew: the grave was about to open.

Mary fell at His feet and said what wounded people have been saying to God ever since.

John 11:32 · KJV
“Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died.”

There is pain in that sentence. Maybe even accusation. You could have stopped this. Jesus does not scold her for saying it. He does not immediately explain the plan. He sees Mary weeping, sees the people around her weeping, and Scripture says He “groaned in the spirit, and was troubled.”

Then comes the shortest verse in the Bible.

John 11:35 · KJV
“Jesus wept.”

Do not rush past those two words because you already know them. Jesus knew Lazarus would be alive again before the day was over. He knew the ending. And He still let the present grief matter.

He did not rush past the grief to get to the miracle.

Knowing the ending does not make the pain unreal.

Sometimes Christians are so eager to talk about heaven, healing, providence, or the good God may eventually bring that we accidentally communicate that today’s tears are somehow unnecessary. Jesus never did that.

He knew resurrection was coming and still wept at the grave. That means Christian hope does not require emotional denial. You can know where the story ultimately ends and still be devastated by the chapter you are living in.

There is no contradiction between faith and tears. There is no spiritual maturity in pretending the empty chair does not hurt. The Savior who promised resurrection is the same Savior who cried before He performed one.

If you are hurting right now

You do not need to make your grief sound more spiritual before you bring it to Jesus. Mary gave Him the unedited sentence: “If thou hadst been here…” You can do the same. Tell Him where it hurts. Tell Him what you wish had happened differently. Tears are not evidence that you have forgotten His promises. Sometimes tears are what faith looks like while you are still waiting to see them fulfilled.

He understands more than pain in general.

Jesus did not weep for an abstract concept called humanity. He stood with Mary. Martha. Lazarus. People with names, histories, and a particular home in Bethany. His compassion was personal.

That matters when your own suffering feels too specific for anybody else to grasp. The God who knows the number of hairs on your head does not reduce your grief to a category. He knows the person you lost. He knows the sentence the doctor said. He knows the anniversary you dread. He knows what you almost texted before remembering there would be no reply.

And Hebrews tells us that He is not untouched by any of it.

Hebrews 4:15 · KJV
“For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities…”

He is touched. Not clinically aware. Not coldly informed. Touched.

What do I do with this tonight?

Start by giving yourself permission to stop explaining your grief and simply bring it to Jesus. Read John 11:21–36 slowly. Notice what Jesus does before Lazarus ever walks out of the tomb. He listens. He sees. He groans. He weeps.

If words are hard, your prayer can be as simple as, “Lord, this hurts, and I need You here.” You do not have to manufacture a lesson from your pain tonight. You do not have to hurry yourself toward being okay.

But you also do not have to grieve alone.

So here is Answer #9, stated plainly: Jesus does not watch your grief from a distance. He has entered human sorrow, stood beside a grave, and wept. He knows what it is to hurt—and He is not ashamed of your tears.

But His tears are not the end of the Christian answer. Sympathy matters. It is just not enough to save us. Jesus walked away from Lazarus’s tomb toward a cross, and there He did more than enter our suffering.
