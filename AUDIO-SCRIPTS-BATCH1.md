# Answer Audio — Batch 1 Ready-to-Record Scripts

These are written to sound like a pastor sitting beside one hurting person, not like a sermon or audiobook. Read them naturally rather than mechanically. Small wording changes in the room are fine; keep the core Answer and KJV Scripture intact.

---

## Answer 01 — Why Does God Feel Far Away?

**File:** `audio/why-does-god-feel-far-away.mp3`

If God feels far away from you right now, I want you to know that you are not the first person of faith to feel that way. Pain has a way of narrowing our vision. We pray and hear silence. We open the Bible and the words can feel flat. Other people talk about God’s presence as if it is obvious, while we are quietly wondering, “Has He really been here at all?”

Here is the short answer I want you to hold onto: **He has always been there.**

Psalm 34:18 says, “The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.”

I love the word *nigh*. Near. Scripture does not describe a broken heart as the place God avoids. It describes it as a place where He draws near.

Now, that does not mean you will always feel Him. Feelings are real, but they do not know everything. You can honestly say, “God, I cannot feel You,” while also saying, “But You have promised not to leave me.” Those two sentences can exist in the same prayer.

So do not try to manufacture a spiritual feeling today. Do something smaller. Tell God one honest sentence about where He feels absent. Then read Psalm 34:18 slowly twice.

You might pray, “Lord, I cannot feel You clearly right now. Help me trust Your promise more than my perception.”

The silence may still feel quiet when you finish. But silence is not the same thing as absence. Let the promise be stronger than the feeling for today.

## Answer 05 — Is It Wrong That I Keep Asking God Why?

**File:** `audio/is-it-wrong-to-ask-god-why.mp3`

If you keep asking God why, you may have wondered whether that question itself means something is wrong with your faith. Maybe you have even felt guilty for asking it again.

I want to tell you plainly: **honest questions are not unbelief.**

Psalm 13:1 records David praying, “How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?”

That is not a polished prayer. David feels forgotten. He feels as though God is hiding. And God allowed that prayer to become Scripture.

The Bible is full of people who asked hard questions. Job asked. David asked. Habakkuk asked. John the Baptist asked questions from a prison cell. Asking why does not necessarily mean you have stopped trusting God. Sometimes the question hurts precisely because you still believe He is good, still believe He is powerful, and cannot understand how what happened fits with what you believe about Him.

There is a difference between asking God a question and walking away from God because you did not get an answer. Biblical lament keeps turning toward Him.

So here is your next step. Write down your hardest why-question exactly as you would say it to God. Do not clean it up. Do not make it sound more spiritual.

Then pray, “God, I have questions I cannot silence. Help me bring them to You instead of carrying them alone.”

You may not get an explanation today. But the absence of an explanation does not mean God is offended by your question. Keep the conversation open. Sometimes faith sounds less like “I understand” and more like “God, I still do not understand—but I am still talking to You.”

## Answer 09 — Does God Actually Know What This Feels Like?

**File:** `audio/does-god-know-what-this-feels-like.mp3`

There is a particular loneliness that comes with suffering when you begin to wonder whether anyone really understands what this feels like. And underneath that can be an even deeper question: “Does God understand, or is He simply watching all of this from a distance?”

The Christian answer is stunningly personal: **He wept with us.**

John 11:35 says simply, “Jesus wept.”

Those may be two of the most important words in the Bible for a hurting person. Jesus knew Lazarus was going to rise. He knew what He was about to do. He knew resurrection was only moments away—and still He stood beside grieving people and wept.

Jesus did not study suffering from heaven. He entered a human body. He knew exhaustion. He knew rejection. He knew betrayal by someone close to Him. He stood at graves. He was misunderstood, falsely accused, beaten, and crucified.

That does not mean your pain suddenly becomes easy. It means you are not bringing it to a God who has never entered sorrow.

So if you are grieving today, you do not have to apologize for the tears. Picture Christ standing at Lazarus’s grave. Resurrection hope did not make Him emotionally detached.

Pray this: “Jesus, thank You for entering human sorrow. Sit with me in mine.”

Sometimes what we need first is not an explanation for why this happened. We need to know that God has not remained safely removed from what hurts. In Jesus, God came near enough to cry at a grave—and near enough to suffer a cross.

He knows what sorrow feels like from the inside.

## Answer 13 — What Do I Do When God Says No?

**File:** `audio/what-do-i-do-when-god-says-no.mp3`

There are some prayers where we know God *can* do what we are asking. That is part of what makes a no so painful. You did not ask because you thought He was powerless. You asked because you believed He could intervene—and He did not answer the way you hoped.

Here is what I want you to remember: **a no is not the end of the story.**

Paul asked God to remove what he called his thorn in the flesh. God’s answer was not removal. In 2 Corinthians 12:9, He said, “My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness.”

That was not the answer Paul requested. But it was not abandonment either.

Jesus Himself prayed in Gethsemane for the cup to pass, while submitting to the Father’s will. The Father’s no was part of something larger than the immediate relief Jesus asked for.

I would be careful, though, about rushing too quickly to find the lesson in your own no. Before you turn the disappointment into a sermon illustration, you may need to grieve it.

Name what you hoped God would do. Say it plainly. “Lord, I wanted You to heal. I wanted You to change this. I wanted the relationship restored. I wanted a different answer.”

Then pray, “Lord, I wanted a different answer. Meet me in the disappointment and give me grace for what is.”

God’s no to one request is not God saying no to you. It is not proof that He stopped loving you. The chapter may hurt deeply, but you do not yet know the whole story.

For today, do not demand that you understand the ending. Ask for sufficient grace for this page.

## Answer 14 — Is This Really the End?

**File:** `audio/is-death-really-the-end.mp3`

When someone you love dies, Christian words can feel both precious and painfully small. You may believe in heaven and still hate the empty chair. You may know every resurrection verse and still ache because the person you love is not here.

Christianity does not ask you to pretend death is small. It tells you death does **not get the final word**.

Jesus said in John 11:25, “I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live.”

Our hope is not ultimately that death is natural, or that memories keep a person alive, or that time makes loss disappear. Christian hope rests on the resurrection of Jesus Christ. He entered death and came out the other side.

That means death is still an enemy—but it is a defeated enemy. Separation still hurts—but for the believer it is not permanent. The grave still receives bodies—but it does not own the ending.

And that gives you permission to do two things at once: grieve deeply and hope deeply.

So today, say the person’s name. Thank God for one specific memory. Do not treat remembering as though it is a failure to move forward. Love is part of why grief hurts.

You might pray, “Jesus, the separation hurts. Hold my grief inside the hope of Your resurrection.”

The resurrection does not erase the funeral. It changes what the funeral can finally mean.

This hurts because death is an enemy. But because Jesus lives, the enemy does not win the last chapter.

## Answer 15 — How Long Am I Allowed to Still Be Sad?

**File:** `audio/how-long-am-i-allowed-to-grieve.mp3`

Maybe enough time has passed that you have started judging yourself for still being sad. Other people have moved on. The meals stopped coming. The calls became less frequent. Life around you looks normal again, and you are wondering why you are not “over it.”

Please hear this: **you are allowed to grieve as long as it takes.**

Psalm 56:8 says, “Thou tellest my wanderings: put thou my tears into thy bottle...”

That is such a tender picture. God is not irritated by tears. He notices them.

The Bible does not give every grieving person the same timetable. There is movement in healthy grief, but movement is not the same thing as speed. Some days you may laugh and feel almost normal. Then a song, a date, a smell, or an empty chair can make the grief feel fresh again.

That does not automatically mean you have gone backward.

Still hurting does not mean you are failing God. Missing someone is not a spiritual defect. Even Jesus wept at a grave.

So your next step today is simple: give yourself permission to be sad without measuring whether you should be over it by now.

Pray, “God, receive the grief I am still carrying. Keep me from shaming myself for missing someone I loved.”

You are not called to forget. You are learning how to carry love, loss, hope, and memory in a life that has changed shape.

Healing may be slow. It may be uneven. That does not make it unreal.

God is not standing over you with a stopwatch. He is near to the brokenhearted.

## Answer 18 — Am I Allowed to Be Furious with God?

**File:** `audio/am-i-allowed-to-be-angry-with-god.mp3`

If you are angry with God, you may be afraid to admit it. Maybe the anger feels disrespectful. Maybe you have been censoring your prayers because you think God would rather hear a calmer version of you.

Here is the short answer: **you are allowed to tell God you are angry.**

Psalm 62:8 says, “Pour out your heart before him: God is a refuge for us.”

Pour it out. God already knows what is there.

Now, that does not mean every conclusion anger reaches is correct. Pain can tempt us to accuse God of things that are not true about Him. But there is a difference between honestly bringing anger to God and letting anger drive you away from God.

The Psalms contain disappointment, confusion, protest, tears, and questions. Biblical lament does not require fake calm before you come into God’s presence.

In fact, sometimes the fact that you are still arguing with God tells us something important: you still believe He is there, you still believe this matters, and somewhere beneath the anger you still believe He should care.

So pray the sentence you have been censoring. Say, “God, I am angry. I do not understand why You allowed this. I wish You had done something different.”

Then add this: “I do not want anger to drive me away from You, so I am bringing it to You.”

You do not have to resolve the entire emotion in one prayer. Keep the conversation open.

Bring the anger *toward* God. Let Him correct what needs correcting, comfort what needs comforting, and hold what you are not yet able to understand.

## Answer 19 — What Do I Even Say to God Right Now?

**File:** `audio/what-do-i-say-to-god-right-now.mp3`

Sometimes pain reaches a point where you do not even know how to pray anymore. You have already said the same things. You are tired of asking. Your thoughts are tangled, and even putting them into sentences feels exhausting.

If that is where you are, hear this: **bring God the real prayer, not the polished one.**

Romans 8:26 says, “The Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought...”

I love that Scripture makes room for a moment when we simply do not know what to pray.

Prayer is not a performance where God grades your vocabulary. He already knows what hurts. He already knows what you are afraid to say. He already knows the anger, confusion, grief, and disappointment underneath the silence.

So maybe your prayer today is one sentence: “God, I do not even know what to say.”

That counts.

Maybe you sit quietly for two minutes because words will not come.

That counts too.

Set a timer for two minutes. Talk to God without editing yourself. If you become silent, stay there. You do not have to fill every second.

You can pray, “God, I do not even know what to say. Hear what I cannot put into words.”

Do not wait until you have a prettier prayer. Bring the one you actually have.

God is not intimidated by the unfinished sentence. The Spirit helps our infirmities precisely because we are weak enough to need help.

Prayer can be tears. Prayer can be a question. Prayer can be silence offered to God.

You do not need impressive words. You need an honest heart turned in His direction.

## Answer 21 — How Do I Forgive Someone Who Never Said Sorry?

**File:** `audio/how-do-i-forgive-someone-who-isnt-sorry.mp3`

Forgiveness becomes especially difficult when the person who hurt you is not sorry. They may deny it happened, minimize what they did, blame you, or simply move on while you are left carrying the damage.

So what does God ask you to do with that?

Here is the short answer: **forgiveness releases vengeance without calling the wrong right.**

Ephesians 4:32 says, “And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you.”

Forgiveness does not mean what happened was harmless. It does not mean you have to forget it. It does not mean there are no consequences. And it does not require the other person to apologize before you can begin releasing your own demand for personal vengeance.

Forgiveness is something you do before God. You say, in effect, “I will not spend the rest of my life trying to make this person pay inside my own heart. I am placing justice in God’s hands.”

That may be a process, especially when the wound is deep.

So do not force yourself to feel forgiving today. Start smaller. Tell God exactly what you are releasing to His justice.

Pray, “Father, I do not want what they did to keep shaping me. Help me release vengeance without denying the truth.”

Forgiveness is not pretending there is no debt. It is refusing to make collecting that debt the organizing purpose of your life.

You can tell the truth about the wound and still refuse to let the wound own you.

That is not weakness. That is freedom beginning to grow.

## Answer 22 — Does Forgiving Them Mean I Have to Let Them Back In?

**File:** `audio/does-forgiveness-mean-reconciliation.mp3`

One reason people resist forgiveness is that they think forgiving someone means giving that person the same access they had before. If the relationship was harmful, unsafe, manipulative, or repeatedly dishonest, that can make forgiveness sound frightening.

Here is an important distinction: **forgiveness is not reconciliation.**

Forgiveness releases personal vengeance before God. Reconciliation rebuilds a relationship. Forgiveness can begin with one willing heart. Reconciliation requires something from both sides—truth, repentance, safety, and rebuilt trust.

John the Baptist told people in Matthew 3:8, “Bring forth therefore fruits meet for repentance.”

In other words, genuine repentance produces fruit. Words matter, but over time repentance becomes visible.

So yes, you can forgive someone and still maintain a wise boundary. You can release vengeance without immediately restoring trust. You can want God’s best for a person without putting yourself back into a destructive pattern.

Trust is not owed on demand. Trust is rebuilt through truthful, consistent behavior.

If someone is pressuring you to make a major reconciliation decision quickly, slow down. Ask a better question: “Has repentance produced trustworthy fruit?”

Pray, “God, give me grace to forgive and wisdom about trust, access, and boundaries.”

Do not confuse a boundary with bitterness. Bitterness wants revenge. A wise boundary recognizes reality.

Forgiveness opens your clenched hand before God. Reconciliation asks whether a relationship can be responsibly rebuilt.

Those are related decisions—but they are not the same decision.
