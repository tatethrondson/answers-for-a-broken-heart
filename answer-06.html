Suffering & Why

Why won’t God just tell me why this is happening?

Sometimes God does not give us the explanation we want. Limited perspective is not proof that He has stopped being wise, good, or present.

About an 8 minute read
Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart
Explore this topic: Why God Allows Suffering →

The short answer

Your view is not the whole story.

Sometimes God does not tell you why. Scripture does not promise a full explanation for every wound. It does tell us that His wisdom is higher than ours, His knowledge is complete, and our inability to see a purpose is not proof that He has stopped being wise, good, or present.

There may be a gap between what you can see and what God knows.

One of the hardest moments Debbie and I ever faced was watching our son Caden being carried toward surgery when he was too young to understand why we were letting it happen. From his perspective, the hallway was frightening and the people who loved him were not stopping it.

From our side of the doors, we knew more. We knew the surgeon. We knew what was damaged. We knew what the procedure was designed to preserve. We could see a future he did not yet have the ability to imagine.

Isaiah 55:8–9 · KJV
“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts.”

The point is not that your pain is childish. The point is that perspective matters. A loving parent can understand something a terrified child cannot, without becoming less loving because the child does not understand.

Not seeing a purpose is not the same thing as proving there is no purpose.

Mystery is not the same as cruelty.

When God does not explain Himself, our minds often fill the silence with the worst interpretation: He does not care, He has forgotten, or there cannot possibly be any good beyond what we can see.

But lack of information cannot carry that much weight. We simply do not possess all the facts. Scripture repeatedly reminds us that God sees history whole while we experience it one moment at a time.

That does not mean every tragedy has a tidy little lesson attached to it. It would be cruel to tell a grieving parent that a child’s death was merely a classroom God designed to teach patience. The Bible does not give us permission to assign specific hidden reasons to someone else’s suffering.

What we can say is smaller and safer: our limited perspective is not the final measure of what is possible for God.

Job never received the map.

Job wanted an explanation. For chapter after chapter, he asked for one. When God finally spoke, He did not hand Job a diagram of everything that had happened behind the scenes. He reminded Job of the difference between the Creator’s knowledge and the creature’s.

Job 38:4 · KJV
“Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.”

That can sound harsh until you understand what God is doing. He is not mocking a hurting man. He is enlarging Job’s field of vision. Job’s suffering was real, but Job’s view was not complete.

Faith does not require you to claim you know the hidden reason. It asks whether the God whose wisdom exceeds yours can still be trusted when your understanding reaches its limit.

Be careful with the phrase “everything happens for a reason.”

People often say that because they are trying to comfort. But to someone in fresh grief it can sound as though we are claiming to know exactly why God allowed the worst thing in their life.

I would rather say this: nothing that has happened is beyond God’s knowledge, beyond His presence, or beyond His ability to redeem. That is a stronger promise because it does not require us to invent an explanation Scripture has not given.

If you cannot make sense of it

You do not have to. Trust and understanding are not identical. You can say, “I do not know why this happened,” while also saying, “My present view is not the whole story.”

What do I do with this tonight?

Release yourself from the pressure to solve the whole story. Ask instead for enough grace for the next faithful step. Read Isaiah 55:8–9 slowly. Then name the part you cannot understand and tell God you are placing that unanswered piece in His hands.

You may never receive a complete explanation in this life. But your inability to see beyond this moment does not mean God is limited to this moment.

So here is Answer #6, stated plainly: your view is not the whole story. You may never receive the explanation you want, but limited perspective is not proof that God has stopped being wise, good, present, or able to redeem what you cannot yet understand.

Questions that usually come next

Why won’t God explain why this happened?

Sometimes Scripture gives a reason for suffering. Often it does not. Job never receives the tidy explanation his friends kept trying to manufacture. Instead, God confronts the limits of Job’s perspective and reminds him that the universe is larger than the portion he can presently see.

That can feel unsatisfying when what you want is an answer. But the biblical response is not, “Stop asking.” It is, “Do not confuse the absence of an explanation with the absence of God.”

“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD.”Isaiah 55:8 · KJV

Would knowing why actually make the pain easier?

Sometimes it would help. Clarity matters. But explanation and comfort are not identical. You can understand why a surgery was necessary and still hurt afterward. You can know why a relationship ended and still grieve it. Some wounds are not healed merely by receiving more information.

That is why Scripture often gives hurting people more than answers. It gives presence, promises, community, lament, and ultimately Christ Himself.

Is God withholding an explanation because He is angry with me?

Not necessarily. Silence is not a reliable indicator of divine displeasure. Faithful people throughout Scripture lived through long stretches when they did not understand what God was doing. Do not build an accusation against yourself from information God has not given you.

If Scripture exposes a sin, respond to it. But do not assume every unanswered question is a coded message of condemnation.

What should I do while I wait for clarity?

Keep the question open without letting it become the only question you ask. Alongside “Why?” ask, “What is faithful today?” “Who do I need?” “What truth do I know?” “What help should I receive?” and “What kind of person do I want this pain to shape me into?”
