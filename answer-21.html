Answer 21 · The People Who Hurt You

How am I supposed to forgive someone who never even said sorry?

Forgiveness is not saying the wound was small. It is refusing to let the person who caused it keep owning your peace.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Relational

The short answer

Forgiving them lets you look like your Father.

Forgiveness does not require the other person’s apology before you can begin. It is an act before God in which you release personal vengeance, refuse to keep collecting the debt in your own heart, and trust God with justice. It does not mean the wrong was acceptable, harmless, or forgotten.

Some apologies never come.

The person moves on. They deny what happened. They minimize it. They rewrite the story. Or perhaps they know exactly what they did and simply do not care.

That makes forgiveness feel impossible because forgiveness seems like giving them something they never earned.

In one sense, that is exactly what forgiveness is.

Grace is never payment for good behavior.

Forgiveness reveals something about God.

Exodus 34:6-7 · KJV
“The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth… forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin…”

Forgiveness is not a side project for God. It is part of the character He revealed about Himself.

And here is the uncomfortable part: you only get to practice forgiveness when someone has actually given you something to forgive.

You cannot imitate God’s forgiveness toward someone who has never wronged you.

The offense becomes the occasion—not for pretending evil is good, but for reflecting a grace that would otherwise remain theoretical.

Forgiveness does not deny the evil.

Joseph is one of Scripture’s clearest pictures.

His brothers betrayed him, sold him, deceived their father, and set in motion years of suffering.

When Joseph finally had the power to retaliate, he did not rewrite history.

Genesis 50:20 · KJV
“But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good…”

He called what they did evil.

Forgiveness did not require softer language.

That matters. Forgiving someone does not mean saying, “It was fine.” It means saying, “It was wrong, but I will not make my whole life a courtroom in which I repeatedly retry this case.”

Forgiveness releases your grip on repayment.

When someone wounds us, part of the heart wants payment.

An apology. Regret. Embarrassment. Consequences. Some visible proof that they understand what they cost us.

Sometimes some of those things are appropriate. Accountability matters.

But there is a form of payment no human being can fully make. They cannot give you back the years. They cannot undo the sentence. They cannot make the memory disappear.

If your peace depends on them finally paying enough, your peace remains tied to their behavior.

Forgiveness is refusing to let someone else’s repentance hold your heart hostage.

Romans 12:19 · KJV
“Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.”

God does not say the debt is imaginary. He says the final repayment belongs to Him.

We forgive from received grace.

Ephesians 4:32 · KJV
“And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

The Christian basis for forgiveness is not that the other person deserves it.

It is that we have received mercy ourselves.

That does not flatten every offense into the same size. It does not erase legal or moral distinctions. It means grace received begins changing the way we carry the debts other people owe us.

If the wound is severe

Forgiveness is never a command to remain in danger, hide a crime, stop an investigation, or remove necessary consequences. You can forgive someone and still report what happened, pursue justice, maintain strong boundaries, and refuse renewed access. The next answer deals directly with that distinction.

Forgiveness may be a decision you revisit.

Sometimes people describe forgiveness as though you say one prayer and never feel the wound again.

Real life is usually messier.

You may decide to release the debt and then wake up angry again next week.

That does not necessarily mean the forgiveness was fake. It may mean the memory is still healing.

You return to the decision: I will not become the collector. God, this belongs to You.

Over time, the emotional temperature can begin to change.

What do I do with this tonight?

Do not begin by trying to feel warm toward the person who hurt you.

Begin by naming the debt.

This is what they did. This is what it cost me. This is what I wish they would give back.

Then tell God: I cannot make them repay what cannot be repaid. I give the final judgment to You. Keep bitterness from becoming the rest of my story.

You may need to pray that more than once.

So here is Answer #21, stated plainly: you can forgive someone even if they never apologize because forgiveness is first an act between you and God, not a reward you hand them for finally behaving well. Name the wrong honestly. Release personal vengeance. Trust God with the ledger. In doing so, you begin to resemble the Father who forgave you.
