Forgiveness and boundaries

What forgiveness does not mean.

Forgiveness is one of the clearest commands Jesus gives His followers. But people sometimes attach requirements to forgiveness that Scripture itself does not attach. Forgiveness is not pretending the wound was small.

Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart

The short answer

Forgiveness means releasing personal vengeance to God and refusing to let bitterness become your master. It does not automatically erase consequences, rebuild trust, restore access, require silence, or produce reconciliation when the other person remains unsafe or unrepentant.

Some of the most wounded people I have counseled were not only carrying what someone did to them; they were also carrying a second burden placed on them by well-meaning Christians: “If you really forgive, you have to act like it never happened.”

That sounds spiritual. It is also too simple.

The Bible calls believers to forgive, but the same Bible tells us to speak truth, confront sin, practice wisdom, protect the vulnerable, involve others when necessary, and recognize the difference between repentance and continued rebellion. Forgiveness matters deeply. So does understanding what forgiveness actually is.

“Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.”Romans 12:19 · KJV

That verse gives us a helpful center. Forgiveness releases the right of personal revenge into the hands of a just God. It says, “I am not going to spend my life trying to make you pay me back.” That is not the same as saying, “Nothing happened.”

Forgiveness releases vengeance. It does not erase wisdom.

1. Forgiveness does not mean the hurt was acceptable.

You do not have to rename evil in order to forgive it. Betrayal is still betrayal. Abuse is still abuse. Lying is still lying. Adultery is still adultery. Theft is still theft. Manipulation is still manipulation. If an action was sinful before you forgave, forgiveness does not make it righteous afterward.

In fact, forgiveness becomes meaningful only when there is something real to forgive. Minimizing the offense is not mercy. It is denial.

2. Forgiveness does not mean forgetting.

People often say, “Forgive and forget,” as though spiritual maturity should erase memory. Human beings do not have a delete key for traumatic or painful experiences. God can heal memory, soften its grip, and keep the past from controlling the future, but remembering what happened is not itself evidence that you have failed to forgive.

Sometimes remembering is part of wisdom. A healed person can remember without living in revenge. Those are not the same thing.

3. Forgiveness does not automatically restore trust.

Forgiveness can be given. Trust is rebuilt.

If someone lied to you repeatedly, the fact that you forgive them does not mean you must immediately believe every new promise. If someone violated a boundary, forgiveness does not require you to remove the boundary the next morning. Trust grows when words and actions become consistently trustworthy over time.

Forgiveness can happen in a moment. Trust usually has to grow through a pattern.

4. Forgiveness does not remove consequences.

David was forgiven by God, and consequences still followed his sin. A person can be genuinely repentant and still need to make restitution, lose a position, face legal consequences, accept supervision, or live with limits created by what happened.

Consequences are not necessarily revenge. Sometimes they are justice. Sometimes they are protection. Sometimes they are part of repentance becoming visible.

5. Forgiveness does not require immediate reconciliation.

Reconciliation is relational restoration. That requires more than one person laying down vengeance. It requires truth, repentance, safety, changed behavior, and a willingness on both sides to rebuild what was broken.

Jesus taught His followers to confront sin, not pretend it did not happen. Matthew 18 includes a process precisely because broken relationships sometimes require clarity, witnesses, and boundaries. Forgiveness should make reconciliation possible where repentance makes reconciliation wise. But forgiveness and reconciliation are not identical.

“Take heed to yourselves: If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him.”Luke 17:3 · KJV

Notice the presence of both truth and forgiveness: rebuke him and forgive him. Biblical grace is not afraid of clarity.

6. Forgiveness does not require giving someone the same access they had before.

Access is not an entitlement. A person can be forgiven and still not be safe enough for the same level of closeness. A former friend may no longer be a confidant. A family member may need limited contact. A ministry leader who violated trust may no longer be qualified for leadership. Someone with a pattern of abuse may require firm distance and outside accountability.

Boundaries are not automatically bitterness. The question is what the boundary is for. Is it designed to punish and control, or is it designed to protect what is healthy, truthful, and safe?

7. Forgiveness does not require silence.

Forgiving someone does not mean you must hide what happened to protect their reputation. There are situations where speaking is necessary—for pastoral accountability, church discipline, protecting children, reporting crime, seeking legal help, obtaining medical care, or warning someone who is genuinely at risk.

Gossip and truthful disclosure are not the same thing. Motive, audience, accuracy, and purpose matter. You can refuse revenge and still tell the truth to the people who need to know it.

So what does forgiveness mean?

Forgiveness means I refuse to make revenge my calling. I release the final accounting to God. I surrender the fantasy that hurting you back will heal what you did to me. I choose not to feed bitterness every day. I ask God for grace to want His righteousness more than my retaliation.

That does not always feel dramatic. Sometimes forgiveness is a decision you make and then have to reaffirm when the memory returns. Sometimes the emotions trail behind the obedience for a while.

Tell the truth about what happened.

Forgiveness begins with reality, not minimization. Name the wound accurately before God.

Release personal vengeance.

You may still pursue proper accountability, but you give the final right to settle the score to God.

Use wisdom about trust and access.

Ask what fruit, repentance, safety, and time would be necessary before closeness could responsibly grow again.

Refuse to let the wound become your identity.

What happened to you matters, but it does not get to become the only story your life tells.

What if they are not sorry?

You can still release your personal vengeance to God even when the offender refuses to repent. Their lack of repentance may change what reconciliation looks like. It may justify continued boundaries. It may mean trust cannot yet be rebuilt. But their refusal to do the right thing does not have to dictate what bitterness does inside you.

If that is the place you are standing, read How am I supposed to forgive someone who never even said sorry? If you are wrestling with whether forgiveness means letting someone back into your life, continue with Does forgiving them mean I have to let them back in?

You can forgive without pretending. You can release vengeance without surrendering wisdom. You can pursue peace without calling danger safe.

Grace and truth belong together.
