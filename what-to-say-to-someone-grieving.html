Helping someone who is grieving

What should I say to someone who is grieving?

You do not need to solve the loss or deliver the perfect spiritual sentence. Most grieving people need something simpler: somebody willing to notice the pain, say the loved one’s name, listen without hurrying, and keep showing up after everyone else has gone home.

Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart

The short answer

Start simple: “I am so sorry. I love you. I am here.” Then listen. If you knew the person who died, share a specific memory. Offer concrete help instead of a vague promise. And remember that your presence will often minister more deeply than your explanation.

Grief makes almost everybody around it feel inadequate. We walk into the hospital room, funeral home, kitchen, or church foyer and suddenly every sentence feels too small. We know we cannot fix what happened, so we start reaching for something profound.

You may not need profound.

You may simply need to be present.

“Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep.”Romans 12:15 · KJV

That verse is wonderfully uncomplicated. It does not say, “Explain to them why they should not be weeping.” It says to weep with them.

Comfort often begins when we stop trying to move the grieving person somewhere else emotionally.

Start with one honest sentence.

If you do not know what to say, say that. “I do not know what to say. I am just so sorry.” That is better than reaching for a theological explanation you cannot possibly know.

Simple

“I am so sorry. I love you, and I am here.”

Honest

“I do not have the right words. I just wanted you to know I care.”

Personal

“I have been thinking about you today. You do not need to answer this.”

Faith-filled without pressure

“I am praying for you. I know that does not make this easy.”

Use the person’s name.

People are sometimes afraid that mentioning the person who died will “remind” the family of their grief. In most cases, they have not forgotten. The name is already in the room.

If you knew the person, say the name. “I was thinking about David today.” “I still remember the way Susan laughed.” “One thing I always appreciated about your dad was…” A memory can be a gift because it tells the grieving person that someone they love is still remembered by someone else.

Ask questions that open a door instead of demanding an answer.

“How are you?” can be difficult because the truthful answer may be enormous. Try something gentler and more specific: “How is today?” “Do you want to talk about her?” “What has been the hardest part this week?” “Would you rather talk or just sit for a while?”

Then do not rush to fill the silence.

Offer help that does not require them to organize you.

“Let me know if you need anything” is kind, but grief makes planning difficult. The grieving person may not know what they need, may not have the energy to ask, or may feel uncomfortable inconveniencing you.

Try something concrete.

Offer a meal with a day attached.

“I would like to bring dinner Thursday. Would 5:30 work, or would it be easier if I just left it at the door?”

Take one ordinary task.

Offer school pickup, yard work, a grocery run, or help with a practical errand.

Give them an easy way to decline.

Specific help should feel supportive, not like another obligation to manage.

Follow up later.

Put a reminder on your calendar for a few weeks from now, when the first wave of attention has passed.

If you share Scripture, let it comfort instead of correct.

The Bible is not too small for grief. It is full of graves, tears, questions, lament, and promises. But Scripture can be used like a balm or like a broom. Do not use a verse to sweep tears out of the room.

Psalm 34:18 is often a better place to begin than an explanation:

“The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.”Psalm 34:18 · KJV

That verse does not demand that the hurting person feel better. It tells them something about where God is in the brokenness.

What if they cry?

Let them cry. Tears are not a conversation failure. You did not necessarily “make it worse.” Sometimes being safe enough to cry with another person is part of being cared for.

You do not need to interrupt the tears with a new subject. Hand them a tissue. Sit there. If appropriate, pray a short prayer. Let sorrow be sorrow.

What if I accidentally say the wrong thing?

Then apologize simply. “I am sorry. That did not come out the way I meant it. I care about you.” You do not have to defend your intentions. Love can survive an imperfect sentence.

The greater danger is often disappearing because you are so afraid of saying something wrong that you say nothing at all.

Keep showing up after the funeral.

The first week can be full of people, food, messages, arrangements, and decisions. Later comes quiet. That is often when the absence feels especially real.

Remember the birthday. Remember the date of the death. Send the text before the first holiday. Invite them again even if they declined the last invitation. Do not assume they are “over it” because they look composed.

The sentence that matters most may be the one you send three months later: “I still remember. I am still here.”

A simple script when you are walking into the room

“I am so sorry. I love you. I do not have an explanation for this, and I am not going to try to make it smaller. I am here to listen, sit, pray, or help with something practical—whatever would serve you best today.”

You do not need to carry their grief for them. You cannot. But you can help make sure they do not carry it alone.

You do not need perfect words.

Say their name. Notice the pain. Offer one practical act of love. Listen longer than feels natural. And then come back later. Grief rarely needs a speech. It needs people who are willing to stay.
