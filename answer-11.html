Answer 11 · When Justice Feels Slow

Does God even care that they’re getting away with it?

Few things make pain heavier than watching the person who caused it appear to move on untouched. Scripture does not ignore that anger.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Honest

The short answer

His silence isn’t His approval.

If the person who hurt you seems fine, that does not mean God has overlooked what happened. Scripture is clear that God sees hidden things, judges justly, and reserves final vengeance for Himself. A delayed verdict is not an acquittal. The courtroom is still in session.

There is a particular kind of pain that comes from being hurt—and then watching the person who caused the hurt seem to prosper.

They sleep. They laugh. They post pictures. They make plans. Maybe people believe their version of the story. Maybe they never apologized. Maybe the consequences landed on you while they kept walking.

And eventually the question becomes less about them and more about God: Are You seeing this?

You are not the first believer to nearly lose your footing over this.

Psalm 73 was written by a man named Asaph, and he begins by admitting that watching wicked people prosper almost broke him.

Psalm 73:2–3 · KJV
“But as for me, my feet were almost gone; my steps had well nigh slipped. For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked.”

That is Scripture—not a confession God edited out because it sounded too bitter. Asaph watched people who did wrong seem to live easier lives than people trying to do right, and he wondered what the point of faithfulness was.

If you have thought, Why am I carrying the consequences while they seem fine? Psalm 73 tells you that question has been prayed before.

What changed was not the past. It was his perspective.

Asaph says trying to understand the situation on his own became too painful. Then something shifted.

Psalm 73:16–17 · KJV
“When I thought to know this, it was too painful for me; Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end.”

He did not suddenly receive a timeline showing when every wrong person would face consequences. He saw something more basic: the story he was looking at was not finished.

What looks permanent from inside one chapter can look very different when the whole book is open.

Fine is not the same thing as finished.

Silence is not a verdict.

When God does not intervene on the timetable we want, it is easy to interpret delay as agreement. Scripture does not let us do that.

A judge who has not yet spoken has not necessarily acquitted the defendant. A case still open is not a case that never mattered. God’s patience and God’s justice can exist at the same time.

Ecclesiastes 12:14 · KJV
“For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil.”

Every secret thing. That includes what nobody else saw. What was denied. What could not be proven to everyone’s satisfaction. What happened behind a closed door. What was said in a conversation that left no record.

God is not confused about the facts.

Why does God tell me not to take vengeance?

Not because what happened was small. Because justice is too serious to entrust to a wounded heart as final judge.

Romans 12:19 · KJV
“Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.”

Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say vengeance belongs to nobody. It says it belongs to God.

That is very different from pretending the wrong did not matter. God asks you to release personal vengeance because He claims the final ledger for Himself.

God is not asking you to call the wrong small. He is asking you to let Him be the final Judge.

Releasing vengeance does not mean refusing legitimate justice.

There is an important distinction between personal revenge and appropriate accountability. If someone has committed a crime, harmed a child, abused a person, stolen, threatened, or placed someone in danger, seeking protection and lawful accountability is not the same thing as vengeance.

You can trust God with the final judgment and still tell the truth. You can forgive in your heart and still cooperate with authorities. You can refuse to hate someone and still recognize that consequences may be necessary.

If you are hurting right now

Do not measure God’s concern by how comfortable the person who hurt you looks today. You are seeing a moment, not the whole ledger. Name what happened truthfully. Pursue legitimate safety and justice where appropriate. And ask God to keep their wrongdoing from turning you into someone consumed by watching for their collapse.

What do I do with this tonight?

Start with Psalm 73. Do not skip Asaph’s anger on the way to his perspective. Let Scripture give you permission to tell God what feels unfair.

Then separate the parts of justice that belong to you from the parts that do not. Telling the truth may belong to you. Reporting wrongdoing may belong to you. Setting a boundary may belong to you. Protecting someone may belong to you.

But living every day with one eye fixed on whether the other person is finally suffering enough was never meant to belong to you.

So here is Answer #11, stated plainly: God’s silence is not His approval. He has not lost the file. He sees what happened, including what nobody else saw, and the last word has not been spoken yet.
