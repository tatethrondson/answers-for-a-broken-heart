Answer 20 · The People Who Hurt You

Why does loving people hurt so much?

The same closeness that makes deep love possible also makes deep wounds possible.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Relational
Written by Tate Throndson · Pastor and author of Answers for a Broken Heart

The short answer

To be loved is to be woundable.

Real relationship requires access. To be deeply known, helped, and loved, you have to let people close enough to matter. That same closeness creates the possibility of being hurt. Vulnerability is not a defect in love. It is part of the cost of genuine relationship.

If you only have 60 seconds

One truth · One Scripture · One prayer · One step
One truth

Love makes us woundable, but being wounded does not mean loving was a mistake.

One Scripture · Psalm 147:3

“He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds.”

Pray this

“God, people have hurt places in me that mattered because I loved them. Please heal what I cannot repair alone.”

One next step

Tell one safe person what hurts instead of rehearsing the wound only in your own head.

If your pain has become dangerous or you are afraid you may hurt yourself, do not carry that moment alone. Use the immediate safety pathway →

Some wounds hurt more because of who caused them.

A stranger can insult you and you may forget it by dinner.

A friend, spouse, parent, child, pastor, or church member can say one sentence and you may still remember exactly where you were standing ten years later.

The difference is access.

The people who can help you most deeply are often the people capable of hurting you most deeply.

Closeness always involves risk.

To be helped in the deepest sense, you have to be known.

Someone has to know what scares you, where you are weak, what happened before, what you hope for, and what you are ashamed to say out loud. That kind of knowing requires an open door.

But the door that lets someone in far enough to bandage a wound also lets them in far enough to cause one.

There is no version of intimacy where you receive all the benefits of being known without accepting any of the vulnerability.

Scripture never pretends relationships are painless.

David knew what it was to be wounded by someone close.

Psalm 55:12-14 · KJV
“For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it… But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance.”

David says the relationship itself intensified the wound.

Jesus knew betrayal by a friend, abandonment by disciples, misunderstanding by family, and rejection by people He came to love and save.

Christianity does not offer a Savior who tells you relational pain should not hurt. It gives you a Savior who knows exactly what betrayal feels like.

Not every relational wound is as simple as it feels at 2:00 a.m.

Sometimes the closer you look, the more complicated the story becomes.

There may have been misunderstanding, bad timing, fear, old wounds colliding with new ones, words heard differently than they were intended, or a conversation that never happened.

Recognizing complexity does not erase your pain.

Your hurt can be valid even when the story is more complicated than your first version of it.

You are allowed to say both: I may not understand everything that happened and what happened genuinely hurt me.

Humility does not require denying your wound.

Some relationships do not explode. They fade.

There is another kind of grief that rarely gets named.

The friendship that slowly disappears.

No fight. No final conversation. Just texts that become less frequent, invitations that stop coming, and a person who used to know your Tuesday afternoons becoming someone you only see in old pictures.

That loss is real too.

Grief does not require a dramatic ending. Sometimes absence is enough.

If someone you loved has hurt you

You are not foolish because the wound matters. The depth of the pain may simply reveal the depth of the relationship. You let someone matter, and that gave them the capacity to affect you. That is not proof you should never trust again. It is proof that trust was real.

The answer is not to build walls around your entire life.

After betrayal, self-protection feels wise.

Nobody gets close again.

Boundaries can be wise. Discernment matters. Trust should not be handed indiscriminately to everyone.

But a life with no vulnerability becomes a life with no intimacy either.

God designed us for relationship—with Him and with one another.

Galatians 6:2 · KJV
“Bear ye one another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.”

You cannot bear another person’s burden from a safe emotional mile away. Community requires closeness.

Love and wisdom belong together.

Jesus loved people deeply, but He did not entrust Himself equally to everyone. John 2 says He “did not commit himself unto them” because He knew what was in man.

That is a helpful distinction.

Christian love does not mean universal access.

You can love someone and still use wisdom about trust. You can remain tender without remaining naive. You can forgive without pretending nothing happened.

That distinction becomes especially important in the next two answers.

What do I do with this tonight?

Name the wound.

Do not minimize it simply because nothing dramatic happened. Ask what was lost and why it mattered.

Then ask another question: What boundary would be wise without turning my whole heart into a wall?

Talk with a trusted person. Pray honestly. If a relationship may be repairable, consider whether a truthful conversation needs to happen. If the relationship is unsafe, wisdom may mean distance.

So here is Answer #20, stated plainly: loving people hurts because genuine love requires vulnerability. The same access that allows someone to know you, help you, and love you also allows them to wound you. Do not mistake that vulnerability for failure. Learn wisdom, use boundaries, grieve what was lost—and do not let one person’s misuse of your trust convince you that love itself was the mistake.
