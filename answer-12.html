Answer 12 · When Justice Feels Slow

Am I just as guilty as the person who hurt me?

“We’re all sinners” is true. But it should never be used to erase responsibility, silence a victim, or pretend every wrong is morally equal.

About an 8 minute read · Biblical · Pastoral · Safety-conscious

The short answer

Your pain and their guilt are not the same conversation.

Every human being needs grace, but universal sinfulness does not make every person equally responsible for a specific wrong. If someone abused, betrayed, stole from, threatened, or harmed you, the fact that you are also imperfect does not erase their culpability. Scripture distinguishes our common need for a Savior from responsibility for particular actions.

Eventually someone may say it: “Well, we’re all sinners.”

The sentence is biblical. The way it is sometimes used is not.

Romans 3:23 · KJV
“For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God.”

All means all. You need grace. I need grace. The person who hurt you needs grace.

But Romans 3:23 is about humanity’s standing before a holy God. It is not a verse designed to flatten every human action into the same moral category.

There are two different conversations.

The first conversation is universal sinfulness: every person has sinned and every person needs Christ.

The second conversation is specific culpability: what did this particular person choose to do to this particular person?

Scripture never requires us to confuse those two.

David was a sinner before Bathsheba, but that did not make Uriah equally responsible for David’s choices. Joseph was a sinner, but that did not make him equally guilty with the brothers who sold him. Paul needed grace, but that did not make the people he persecuted equally culpable for his persecution.

Your need for grace does not erase their responsibility for what they did.

Humility is not carrying guilt that belongs to someone else.

There is a kind of false humility that sounds spiritual because it keeps saying, “I’m sure I did things wrong too.” Sometimes that is appropriate self-examination. Sometimes it becomes a way of avoiding the truth.

If someone lied about you, you do not become equally guilty because you once told a lie somewhere else. If someone abused you, your own imperfections do not make you responsible for their abuse. If someone stole from you, the fact that you have needed forgiveness in other parts of life does not make the theft mutual.

Grace tells the truth before it offers forgiveness.

You are allowed to name a wrong without claiming to be sinless.

Seeking safety is not vengeance.

This matters especially when the harm is ongoing. Christianity does not require a person to remain in danger in order to prove they forgive.

If you are in an unsafe situation right now, get safe first.
Contact appropriate local authorities, emergency services, a trusted person, or a qualified professional when necessary. Do not use teaching about patience, forgiveness, or God’s timing as a reason to remain accessible to someone who is actively dangerous.

Reporting a crime is not the same as revenge. Seeking a protective order is not the same as hatred. Telling a church leader, counselor, employer, school, or authority what actually happened can be part of appropriate accountability.

Romans 12 tells us not to take personal vengeance. Romans 13 immediately describes civil authority as having a legitimate role in restraining wrongdoing. God can use systems of accountability.

Then why is God patient with wrongdoers?

2 Peter 3:9 · KJV
“The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.”

God’s patience is not indifference. It is mercy aimed toward repentance.

Saul stood on the wrong side of Stephen’s death. Acts tells us he consented to it. God did not strike Saul dead that day. He gave him time—and later stopped him on the road to Damascus and transformed the persecutor into Paul.

That story can be difficult if you are the person waiting for justice. God may give a wrongdoer time to repent. But their possible future repentance does not require your present exposure to danger. It does not mean you must restore trust prematurely. It does not make what happened small.

God’s patience with them is not God’s accusation against you.

You can leave the final ledger with God and still have boundaries.

Trusting God with judgment does not require pretending trust has been rebuilt. Forgiveness, reconciliation, access, and trust are related ideas, but they are not identical.

You can refuse personal revenge while still saying, “You may not do that to me again.” You can pray for someone’s repentance without putting yourself back within their reach. You can want redemption for them and safety for yourself at the same time.

If you are carrying someone else’s guilt

Ask yourself whether you have been taking responsibility for choices you did not make. Confess your own sin where it is real. Make amends where you actually caused harm. But do not invent guilt as the price of appearing humble. God can handle an honest distinction between what belongs to you and what belongs to someone else.

What do I do with this tonight?

Write two columns if you need to. In one, name what is genuinely yours: words you regret, decisions you need to own, areas where you need forgiveness. In the other, name what was done to you that you did not choose.

Do not move items from the second column into the first just because carrying their guilt feels easier than admitting how badly you were hurt.

If safety or legal accountability is involved, talk to someone qualified to help you take the next appropriate step.

So here is Answer #12, stated plainly: being a sinner yourself does not put you on equal moral ground with someone who specifically harmed you. Own what is yours. Refuse what is not. Get safe when necessary. Seek legitimate accountability. And leave the parts of the ledger you cannot carry in God’s hands.
